One may feel they are getting ahead in life, but what does that really mean without time?

How much time has passed is, in practice, only an estimate made by the brain, often distorted by distraction and other worldly preoccupations. We humans are so obsessed with the future, so aware that we cannot escape death, that time is born in our attempt to defy that truth. Time becomes an emergent property of our abstraction of the universe—the platform in which we are instantiated—arising from our realization of our own mortality, our eventual “game over.”

We have learned to quantify this abstraction for day-to-day use, and that practical bookkeeping is what most people call time. We then turn around and look at the universe with this derived concept installed as a fundamental axiom—a restriction, almost a filter, through which everything must pass. Meanwhile, our collective ideas evolve. We learn and reinforce patterns at every scale: as cells, as individuals, as societies, as a species. Within those layers, we can do whatever needs to be done.

In that sense, time is a manifestation of our imagination. Our large, complex societies would collapse without the concept; trains would not arrive, markets would not open, and agreements could not be honored. The pursuit of quantifying time has been continuous; even today, we chase ever finer precision. More precision means more data points in our simulated models of reality—but that is largely for engineering purposes, not necessarily for understanding being itself.

We could, in principle, attempt to quantify something as intangible as love—correlating neural firing patterns, heart rhythms, and other physiological signals, then training neural networks on that data. We might produce a model that predicts “love” with high accuracy. But would that be a true depiction of love, or only a useful shadow of it? At my work we sell digital experiences—the good and expensive kind—which we quantify as collections of processes. Yet we know that the experience itself is more than the process description.

The past is inaccessible to us directly, and the future remains uncertain. All we ever actually have is the present moment. Even there, physics reminds us that we cannot explicitly pin down a particle at a precise position and time—the uncertainty principle forbids it. This feels almost like a paradox in our interpretation of time and therefore of “the universe.”

We picture the universe as being in constant motion and, in our current physical theories, imagine it “updating” itself every Planck time. That is the smallest time scale that currently makes sense in our models; by extension it implies a smallest meaningful length scale as well. In this picture, everything—ourselves included—is continually re-instantiated, tick after tick, from these fundamental bits of the universe, whatever they ultimately turn out to be. We still do not know what exactly existed one Planck time after the big bang, only that our equations hint at some primordial informational substrate.

If we are biological computers—and in many respects we are—then time is simply the duration of the computer’s operation as it runs its program. Somewhere along the way, this computer became sentient. What that truly means is a question for another day. For now, it is enough to note that time, as we experience and use it, sits at the intersection of biology, imagination, and our refusal to look away from our own finitude.
